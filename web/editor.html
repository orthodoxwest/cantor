    A short guide

    GABC in seven steps

    GABC is the plain-text way of writing square notation that Gregorio and GregoBase use, and that every score in this site's library is written in. You write the words, and after each syllable, in brackets, the notes it is sung to. Each example below is engraved as written; Try it puts it in the editor (undo brings back what you had).

    1. Words, then notes

    Each syllable is followed by its notes in brackets. The syllables of one word run together; a space starts a new word. The first bracket of the score holds the clef.

    2. Pitches are letters

    The letters a to m are the lines and spaces of the staff, from below it upward: d is the bottom line, e the space above it, and so on to j on the top line; a to c lie below the staff and k to m above it. The clef says where do sits: c4 puts it on the fourth line (so j is do), c3 on the third (h), and f3 puts fa on the third.

    3. Several notes, one syllable

    Letters written together in one bracket make a neume, sung on the one syllable and drawn joined. Rising notes join into a podatus (fg), falling ones into a clivis (hg), and up-and-down into a torculus (ghg). A capital letter is a diamond note, for quick falling runs: hGF. A / inside the bracket breaks the neume into pieces with a small gap (// or a space gives a bigger one).

    4. Bars and breath

    Bars go in their own brackets: (,) a quarter bar for a breath, (;) a half bar for a short pause, (:) a full bar at the end of a phrase, and (::) the double bar at the end of a piece. A * in the words marks a division: in an antiphon, where the choir takes over from the cantor; in a psalm verse, the pause in the middle.

    5. Marks on the notes

    A sign after a letter changes how it is sung or drawn: . a dot that doubles the note, _ an episema that lengthens it a little, v a virga (a note with a stem), w a quilisma (the jagged note in a rising group), ~ a liquescent, a small note where the voice glides through a consonant such as l, m or n, and s a stropha, often three on one pitch. A letter and x put a flat on that line or space, to the end of the word or the next bar: under c4, (ixi) sings ti flat.

    6. The header

    A file can start with lines that describe it, each key:value;, ended by a line holding only %%. name names the piece (Gregorio expects at least that), and mode its mode, which is printed over the initial. The first letter of the words becomes the large initial; as in the printed books, the letter after it is written as a capital too, so WHere. A line starting with % is a comment. The editor engraves a score with or without a header.

    7. Rubrics and signs

    Words the book prints in red, such as a direction or a verse number, go between <c> and </c>; empty brackets after them, (), make them a syllable with no notes. <i> and </i> set words in italic, and the two can be combined: <c><i>Bow.</i></c>(). <sp>V/</sp> and <sp>R/</sp> print the versicle and response signs ℣ and ℟, and <c>✠</c> the red cross where the sign of the cross is made. Signs like these go just before the first sung word, in the same syllable.
